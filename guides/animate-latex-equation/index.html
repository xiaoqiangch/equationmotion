How to animate a LaTeX equation

There are three real options in 2026. They differ less in output than in how much machinery you are willing to run before you see a frame.

Option 1 — Manim

Manim is the engine behind 3Blue1Brown, and it is still the only option that gives you a full geometric vocabulary: Transform, TransformMatchingTex, updaters driven by a ValueTracker, camera moves, 3D surfaces. If your explanation depends on a picture changing shape — a vector field rotating, an area rearranging into a rectangle — Manim is not overkill, it is the minimum.

The cost is a stack: Python, Cairo, Pango, and a TeX distribution for MathTex. On a clean macOS machine that is a 1–2 GB install (MacTeX alone is most of it) and a decent chance of a font or dvisvgm detour. Once it runs, the workflow is a Python file, a render command and a wait.

Choose it when: the motion is the explanation, or you need the same scene re-rendered dozens of times as the script changes.

Option 2 — a motion-graphics app

After Effects, and the browser-native editors that copy it, will happily animate text; LaTeX usually arrives through a plugin or an image import, which means the equation is a picture, not text. That is fine for title cards and terrible for a derivation, because changing one symbol means re-rendering the picture.

Choose it when: the equation is one element inside a broader composition with footage, grading and audio.

Option 3 — a browser renderer

Type the lines, get frames. Under the hood this is MathJax typesetting to SVG paths plus a canvas that reveals each glyph in reading order — a "write-on" that looks like a hand writing fast, without the wobble that makes handwriting fonts look fake.

The honest limitation: it does not morph one equation into another term by term. If you need a²+b²=c² to slide itself into a²=c²−b², that is Manim's TransformMatchingTex and nothing else does it as cleanly.

The three-step workflow that covers most cases

  1. Split the derivation into lines. One line per animation step. Lines shorter than about 50 characters read best; anything longer gets shrunk to fit.
  2. Pick the reveal. Write-on for derivations, fade for statements, slide for lists of conditions.
  3. Export frames, not a video. A transparent PNG sequence drops into Premiere, DaVinci Resolve or After Effects without a re-encode, and you keep the alpha.
